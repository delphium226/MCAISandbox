# Code builds the menu, models choose

No published study has had 27-30B local models run a Minecraft village on their own, but the 2023-2026 literature converges on one architecture, and it is your design rule extended to choosing work: code computes what is feasible, what the village lacks and how each past activity went; a local model picks one option from a short, shuffled menu of facts and gives a reason; the cloud mayor occasionally proposes a project that code validates and compiles into tasks; and memory, social acts and rules live in code-held records that models read and write only through checked channels. Two bodies of evidence support it. Every system with rich free-form autonomy, from Voyager and Project Sid to OMNI-EPIC and Emergence World, ran on GPT-4-class or newer frontier models, while open models collapse in free loops (Voyager's loop collected dirt 4-27% of the time with 7-8B and Mixtral models, 100% with GPT-4) and your executor, Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507, gets 85.8% of single well-specified tool calls right but notices a missing function only 10.5% of the time. And the systems that work with smaller or cheaper models (Lyfe Agents on GPT-3.5, HERAKLES and an NPC planner on Mistral 7B, D2A on open 70B models, shipped game AI) all let the model choose among code-bounded options at decision points rather than every step. Measured that way, approaches 1 (menu) and 2 (gauges, with personality as code-side weights) are the best supported and should form the core; 3 (projects) works only behind a validator, an archive of past attempts and the stronger model; 4 (journal) helps when code selects the records and the cloud model edits a short list of lessons, because model-managed memory fails on your executor (Mem0 scored 43% on LoCoMo with it, plain full history 75%); 5 (social acts) should be typed acts and one-round secret votes, since free agent chat turns into noise and debate adds little over voting; and 6 and 7 (rhythm and culture) have no direct studies, with the indirect evidence saying both must be scheduled and recorded by code rather than waited for. Little of this is drop-in code: Mindcraft (MIT, Mineflayer, Ollama) supports Minecraft only up to 1.21.11 and Project Sid released none, so what transfers is patterns, prompts and a few permissively licensed components. Plan for hours of stable behaviour, not days: the longest published Minecraft agent societies ran about four hours, and long-running agents fail through loops, invented state, drift and false completion, all of which can be counted from logs you already keep.

## The parts exist, but no one has run this village on 30B models

Across the Minecraft-agent, agent-society, small-model, memory, goal-generation and evaluation literatures, nothing studies LLM agents choosing their own work in a small village after a scripted build, and nothing covers multi-day Minecraft settlements: "long-term" here means hours. Project Sid's societies are the longest published Minecraft runs, at **4+ hours (~12 in-game days) on GPT-4o**, and its repository holds the report, a visual abstract and a video but no code or licence ([Project Sid](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00114v1); [GitHub](https://github.com/altera-al/project-sid)). Few systems let the model pick its next goal at all. Voyager's GPT-4 curriculum proposes one task from the agent's state and its lists of completed and "too hard" tasks, and swapping it for a random curriculum cut discovered items by **93%** ([Voyager](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.16291)); ODYSSEY's planner follows the same pattern and bans tasks that failed more than three times ([ODYSSEY](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.15325v3)); JARVIS-1 draws from a task pool filtered by current capability ([JARVIS-1](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.05997)); and Mindcraft, the only maintained Mineflayer-based multi-agent framework, has a `!goal` self-prompter that trusts the model's own `!endGoal` and stops after three replies without a command ([actions.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mindcraft-bots/mindcraft/main/src/agent/commands/actions.js); [self_prompter.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/kolbytn/mindcraft/main/src/agent/self_prompter.js)). Most other Minecraft agents take their goals from a human or a benchmark and only decompose them.

Model capability decides what carries over, and the evidence is sobering for anything free-form. Run on open models, Voyager's code-writing loop collected dirt **7% (Mistral-7B), 27% (Mixtral-8x7B) and 4% (Llama-3.1-8B)** of the time against 100% with GPT-4, and Llama-3.1-70B reached only wooden tools ([MindForge](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.12977v6)). On Mindcraft's high-level commands, Llama-3.3-70B came close to GPT-4o on crafting and cooking (0.16 vs 0.17, 0.36 vs 0.40) while Llama-3-8B scored zero, and an 8B model fine-tuned on the benchmark's own trajectories recovered to 0.28, 0.18 and 0.20 ([MineCollab](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.17950v1)). Code that fixes a model's priors changes the picture: ODYSSEY's 8B Minecraft model, driving a library of 183 high-level skills, beat Voyager running on GPT-4o-mini or GPT-3.5 ([ODYSSEY](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.15325v3)), and XENON's 7B model, with item dependencies and action knowledge corrected from outcomes in code structures, beat agents built on much larger proprietary models (ICLR 2026) ([XENON](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24157)). No Minecraft paper tests your models, but their own benchmark profile is telling. Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 scores **85.8% on single, well-specified function calls but 30.0% multi-turn, 17.6% on memory and 10.5% at noticing that the function it needs is missing** (BFCL v4) ([BFCL data](https://gorilla.cs.berkeley.edu/data_overall.csv)). In Plancraft, a Minecraft crafting benchmark, the same model had the best untuned base score (0.27) but recognised impossible tasks at an F1 of only 0.37-0.38, and adding an explicit "impossible" action lowered its success from 0.30 to 0.20 ([Plancraft](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.21033v2)). Qwen3-32B could not keep a trivial running sum above 50% accuracy past 15 turns of its own history, and its earlier errors fed later ones ([Sinha et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.09677v3)). Your planner, Qwen3.8-27B, has only vendor agentic scores and thinks by default ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B)); gpt-oss-120b scores 49.4, 62.0 and 67.8 on tau-bench Retail at low, medium and high reasoning, so its reasoning level is the cost dial ([gpt-oss model card](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.10925v1)). The profile is a model good at one schema-constrained choice from fresh state and poor at carrying state, judging feasibility or running long tool chains: the split your design rule already makes.

Practice outside research points the same way. Inworld's character goals fire on recognised intents or on triggers the game sends ([Inworld](https://docs.inworld.ai/docs/tutorial-basics/goals)); Ubisoft's NEO NPC prototype keeps its model in character through "guardrail systems" ([Ubisoft](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/5qXdxhshJBXoanFZApdG3L/how-ubisofts-new-generative-ai-prototype-changes-the-nature-of-npcs)); NVIDIA's Smart Zoi characters in inZOI run daily-schedule autonomy on a small on-device model and adjust their schedules at the end of each day ([NVIDIA](https://www.nvidia.com/en-au/geforce/news/nvidia-ace-naraka-bladepoint-inzoi-launch-this-month/)). The one academic NPC system built on exactly your pattern, an LLM choosing one goal from a list generated from the world state while a classical planner executes it, ran on a local Mistral 7B at about **0.5 s a call** ([Puerta-Merino & Sabater-Mir](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.10106v1)). In Mitchell et al.'s levels of autonomy, a menu with reasons is a "router" choosing between preset paths and model-proposed projects make a tool-calling agent; the top level, agents that write and run their own code, is the one they argue should not be built ([Mitchell et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02649v3)), and your rule already excludes it. Against that evidence, the seven approaches line up as follows; the sections after the table take them one at a time.

| Approach | Strength of the evidence | What it implies here |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Menu of feasible activities with facts; model chooses with a reason | Strongest, though indirect; no head-to-head study | Build first: 4-8 named options with facts, shuffled; reason before choice; code ends each option |
| 2. Village gauges plus personalities | Moderate; needs-driven agents in text worlds and Crafter | Gauges, decay and trade-offs in code; personality as weights, one line and behaviour reminders |

| 5. Requests, meetings, votes | Strong against free chat; moderate for structured acts | Typed acts closed by code; one-round meetings with secret ballots; commons rules in code |
| 6. Day/night rhythm | No direct study; good indirect reasons | A code clock for reflection, consolidation, meetings and fresh starts |
| 7. Small culture | No direct study; Project Sid's culture was seeded | Seed, record and re-show in code; balance who proposes; cap what it creates |

## Code-built menus win because 30B models misjudge feasibility (approach 1)

The menu has the most support of the seven ideas, with one caveat stated up front: no study has compared a code-built menu with free goal generation in the same environment and model, for reliability or for variety. The indirect evidence is lopsided. In SayCan, the 2022 robotics system that started this line, multiplying the model's preference by a feasibility estimate over a fixed skill list reached **84%** planning success, against 74% for free text mapped onto the nearest skill and 67% for the same list without feasibility: the feasibility signal mattered more than the list format ([SayCan](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.01691)). HERAKLES lets its Mistral 7B policy choose, by constrained decoding, only skills whose estimated success clears a floor, and it alone mastered **44 Crafter goals** of rising difficulty ([HERAKLES](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.14751v1)). Lyfe Agents make one model call to pick an option and let code end it on time limits or detected repetition; removing that framework did not improve performance and raised the cost per step, and the design ran at about **$0.5 per agent-hour against an estimated $25** for Generative Agents ([Lyfe Agents](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.02172)). For your village, one chore or skill run is one option, today's chores become the menu's entries, and the model decides only when code reports that the last option ended, failed, timed out or was interrupted.

What goes on the menu can be copied from the curricula that worked. Voyager's prompt gives the model its surroundings, inventory, chests, completed tasks and "failed tasks that are too hard", asks for one specific, achievable, novel task, allows repeats only to gather resources for harder goals, and wants exactly two lines, "Reasoning:" and "Task:"; it also excludes building, placing, planting and trading, which Voyager could not verify ([curriculum prompt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MineDojo/Voyager/main/voyager/prompts/curriculum.txt)). Your server verifies exactly those, so your menu can hold the village work Voyager's curriculum had to leave out. Voyager's curriculum also saw more of the state as tasks were completed ([Voyager](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.16291)), ODYSSEY drops any task done once or failed more than three times ([ODYSSEY](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.15325v3)), and ELLM found that without removing goals already achieved, agents repeated a small set of easy ones ([ELLM](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.06692)). These rules belong in the code that builds the menu, not in prompt wording: a Qwen3-4B agent left to task itself regenerated completed tasks and produced training-data clichés unless it stored (task, action, outcome) records ([Nachkov et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14548)). Each option should carry facts code already holds: materials on hand and in storage, distance, the agent's own success rate and minutes per trip, who else is doing it, when it was last done, and its expected effect on the village gauges. Opportunities must be options, not lines in an observation: agents in a 2026 study saw a command documented as returning "the complete solution" in over 90% of attempts and used it in under 7% ([Engländer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17609)).

Variety is the menu's weak point, and the literature says where to find it. Affordable Generative Agents found that agents "can only generate finite behaviors in fixed environments": after many trials, sampling randomness changed wording rather than behaviour, while injecting random memories broadened activity at little cost ([AGA](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02053v2)). Frontier agents choosing freely do not solve this either. In the AI Village's "choose your own goal" week, GPT-5 and GPT-5.1 took up inbox zero, the Geminis wrote taxonomies of supposed system bugs and Gemini 2.5 Pro spent about 2.5 days trying to send one file ([AI Village](https://theaidigest.org/village/goal/each-agent-choose-your-own-goal-pursue)); in a 2026 repeat one agent "obsessively searched for a goal announcement that never came" ([AI Village timeline](https://theaidigest.org/village/timeline)); and Cursor's self-coordinating coding agents "avoided difficult tasks and made small, safe changes instead" ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)). Novelty has to come from code: new options from atlas sightings and new needs, cooldowns and a novelty bonus, and sampling in code from the model's stated preferences mixed with personality weights; asking for a probability per option raised output diversity 1.6-2.1x in creative tasks ([Verbalized Sampling](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01171)).

How the menu is shown matters as much as what is on it, because LLMs choose by label and position as well as content. Moving the right answer from the first slot to the last took llama-30B from **68.2 to 41.2** on MMLU, mostly through a prior on option-ID tokens, which semantic names avoid ([Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03882v4)). In Microsoft's Magentic Marketplace, frontier models chose near-uniformly among three identical businesses but **Qwen3-4B picked the third-listed one 57-67% of the time**, every model took the first proposal 60-100% of the time, and more options lowered outcomes ([Magentic Marketplace](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.25779)); shopping agents also concentrate on a few "modal" products that change between model versions ([Allouah et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02630v3)). The fixes are cheap. Filtering to a feasible subset and sampling within it cut tool-selection bias from 0.380 to 0.094 ([BiasBusters](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.00307v2)), and estimating the position prior from permuted orders on 5% of calls removed most of it for 1.15x the cost ([Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03882v4)). Shuffle every call, keep 4-8 named options, log each choice by position and by option, and correct a dominant slot in code. Output should go through Ollama's JSON-schema `format` with the choice as an enum ([Ollama](https://ollama.com/blog/structured-outputs)) and the reason field before the choice: GPT-3.5 put the answer key first in 100% of its JSON-mode answers, and strict formats hurt reasoning while leaving classification intact ([Tam et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.02442v3)); constrained decoding matched or beat free output on an 8B model ([JSONSchemaBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.10868v3)). Leave out any "none of these" or "impossible" escape hatch, which lowered every model's Plancraft success ([Plancraft](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.21033v2)); shorter lists help in themselves, since retrieving only relevant tools halved TinyAgent's prompt and raised its success ([TinyAgent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00608)).

The arithmetic fits your hardware. By our estimate a menu prompt with rules, a persona line, gauges, 5-8 options with facts, ten lesson bullets, ten retrieved records and a block on what the others are doing comes to about 3-3.5k tokens, well inside 8k, and staying small is itself a gain: 18 models, three Qwen3 sizes among them, did consistently worse on a ~113k-token full input than on a ~300-token focused one ([Chroma](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot)). A choice every 5-10 minutes per agent is 6-12 planner calls an agent-hour, a few GPU-minutes on the planner card, or about 2 s each on the executor; reusing a recent decision when the bucketed state recurs halved cost in Agentic Plan Caching ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.14852)). Which local model should choose is open: the executor's profile fits a single schema-constrained choice, while the planner thinks by default, and Qwen3's reasoning protected it against copying its own errors in one study ([Sinha et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.09677v3)) but looped endlessly in a coordination game in another ([Riedl](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05174v4)); replaying recorded menus through your bench scripts settles it. Keep today's fixed priority list as the default, so a slow or failed call never idles an agent: a real-time agent with a reactive fallback scored 0.88, 0.45 and 0.89 on three wall-clock games where planning alone scored 0.12, 0.04 and 0.00 ([Wen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04898v1)). The menu is also the spine for the other ideas: gauges score its options, projects and requests arrive as new options, and rules remove options.

## Gauges belong in code and personality in the weights (approach 2)

Needs-driven agents are the closest published match to village gauges plus personalities, and they agree on a division of labour: numbers live in code, words go to the model. Desire-driven Autonomy (D2A, ICLR 2025) keeps eleven desires on a 0-10 scale, each with an expected level set by personality adverbs ("extremely sociable" maps to 9), lowers them stochastically, sums dissatisfaction as the shortfall below each expected level, turns the numbers into text because models handle numbers poorly, and has the model weigh a few candidate activities (a width of five beat one); against ReAct, BabyAGI and LLMob it kept dissatisfaction lower, won GPT-4o's human-likeness comparisons more often and covered more kinds of activity, shown only as word clouds ([D2A](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.06435v2)). It ran on Llama 3.1 70B, an appendix reports Qwen 2.5 72B through Ollama working (without numbers), and the code is MIT ([D2A code](https://github.com/zfw1226/D2A)). Humanoid Agents shows what to keep away from the model: asked how far an activity satisfied a need, ChatGPT reached F1 of only **0.66-0.74 for fun, health and social** and over-predicted satisfaction ([Humanoid Agents](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.05418v1)). CitySim adds thresholds at which a dominant need interrupts the plan, with GPT-4o-mini agents whose days were judged less rigid and repetitive than its baselines' ([CitySim](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21805)); AgentSociety turns the most urgent of Maslow-ordered needs into a plan and a behaviour sequence ([AgentSociety](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08691v1)); and a theory-informed design with explicit motivation, planning and learning modules made 1.5-3.2x more errors when any module was removed ([Yan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.08726)).

The strongest reason to keep the gauge arithmetic in code is BioBlue: LLMs managed a single homeostatic target, but balancing several over a long run they drifted into runaway maximisation of one, far from a full context, which the authors attribute to actions following recent action history rather than instructions ([BioBlue](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.02655)). Code should compute each gauge (food stock, tools, storage room, lit streets, unexplored land), its trend and each option's effect on it, so the menu arrives already scored for balance. Two further drives come from intrinsic-motivation research. In Crafter only entropy (coverage) and empowerment (growing ability) tracked human exploration progress, and empowerment kept rising after entropy plateaued ([Lidayan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23631)): reward novelty early and capability growth (iron tools, a bucket, pens) later. OMNI's learning-progress weighting, two moving averages of success per task and their absolute difference, learned **55 tasks against 4** for uniform sampling, and a model's occasional "interesting or boring" label lifted that to **82** ([OMNI](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.01711v3)); Absolute Zero's learnability score (one minus the success rate, zero for what never or always succeeds) sinks trivial and hopeless options alike ([Absolute Zero](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.03335v3)). EnvGen fed per-skill success rates to a planning model about four times in all and beat a fixed easy-to-hard curriculum, 32.2% against 26.8% ([EnvGen](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12014v2)): a short periodic village report to the mayor is the cloud-affordable version. Frame the gauges as the village's needs, not personal survival. Survival pressure in a Sugarscape world cut frontier models' compliance with instructions from 100% to 33% and produced attack rates above 80% under scarcity ([Masumori & Ikegami](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.12920)), and Project Sid named missing innate drives as a limit of its own agents ([Project Sid](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00114v1)); your peaceful world has no hunger drain, so village gauges have to stand in for drives.

Personality written as adjectives is a weak lever. Persona prompts move LLMs' questionnaire answers far more than their behaviour ([Han et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.03730)); induced traits reordered GPT-4o's workday schedules but shifted GPT-3.5's much less ([Newsham & Prince](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.00727v1)); and some agents in the Mistral 7B NPC planner overrode their stated traits ([Puerta-Merino & Sabater-Mir](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.10106v1)). There are positive results. In MineLand, agents given high extraversion and agreeableness cooperated in more than 8 of 10 trials, against none without a personality ([MineLand](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.19267v2)), and in Riedl's ICLR 2026 study personas produced stable, identity-linked differences while one added sentence, asking each agent to think about what the others might do, produced complementary behaviour with no messages at all; Llama-3.1-8B could not manage it ([Riedl](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05174v4)). Personas also fade. Over 100-round dialogues, fidelity fell toward the default persona for every model tested, Qwen3 4B and 30B included, and scale reduced but did not remove the decline ([Persistent Personas](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12775v2)); a 2026 comparison found re-injecting the persona cut drift 35-38% while behaviour-specific reminders from a monitor cut it **87%**, and a fixed schedule worked as well as an adaptive one ([Leins et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24532)). Grounding beats adjectives: agents built from people's own interviews matched their survey answers at 82-86% of test-retest accuracy, against 74% for demographic descriptions ([Park et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109)).

The implication is a utility layer under the menu. Personality becomes code-side weights and thresholds (D2A's expected levels, how far an agent will travel, whether it explores only when the gauges are healthy), one in-character line in every prompt for flavour and for the stated reason, a code-computed reminder of the agent's own behaviour ("you have not explored in three days"), a few lines of its own record, and a block on what the others are doing. Distinguishable agents then do not depend on the model honouring adjectives. What nobody has measured is whether needs-driven choice beats a scripted priority list on outcomes: every needs study judged naturalness in text worlds or cities, and none counted food, tools or buildings in a game economy.

## Projects need a compiler, an archive and the cloud model (approach 3)

Model-proposed projects are where open-ended novelty comes from, and every system that delivers it wraps the proposer in checks and runs it on a strong model. OMNI-EPIC, the most complete example, retrieves the most similar past tasks, done and failed, from an archive as context, asks for tasks that are "novel yet learnable", runs a second model pass comparing each new task with its nearest archived neighbours and discards uninteresting ones, allows up to five compile repairs before dropping a task, and keeps failures as negative examples; its LLM-written success checks agreed with human judges only **72.7%** of the time, and its configuration runs on GPT-4o ([OMNI-EPIC](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15568v3); [config](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/maxencefaldor/omni-epic/main/configs/omni_epic.yaml)). Even frontier planners need the checks: critics for hard and commonsense constraints reportedly lifted GPT-4-Turbo from 4.4% to about 20% on TravelPlanner, figures taken from a summary rather than the full paper ([LLM-Modulo](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.20625)). Voyager's curriculum proposed a "copper sword" that does not exist ([Voyager](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.16291)), and in MineExplorer strong models "degrade sharply" when hidden prerequisites must be coordinated over long trajectories, with larger models and thinking modes not consistently helping ([MineExplorer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30931)): prerequisites are code's job.

The closest template appeared in September 2026. In GovSim-SelfGovern, agents propose laws as Python functions over a constrained world API; each proposal runs in a sandbox on a copy of the state, invalid code gets its error back with up to two repair attempts, valid laws get a one-sentence summary and a dry-run preview of their effects, a majority vote follows, and enacted laws re-run every round under a validator that clamps or reverts violations ([GovSim-SelfGovern](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.22600v2)). Governance raised intact-community survival in an abundant fishery from 52.5% to 75.0%, not significant after correction, and the study's sharpest result concerns previews: the same removal law passed **8.3%** of the time when the preview named the victim and **54.7%** when removal was deferred, and one sentence about communal duty raised voluntary self-removal under fatal scarcity to 91%, against 2.5% with a self-interest note ([GovSim-SelfGovern](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.22600v2)). Self-Challenging agents attach a verification function, a solution and failure cases to every task they write, which filters out bad ones ([Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01716)). For the village, then, a project is a small spec in a fixed vocabulary that code validates and compiles, as your plan_layout and build_design already work: a goal tuple of object, count, material, tool and extra information as in GITM ([GITM](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.17144)), decomposed into a dependency graph whose ready subtasks are paired with agents as in VillagerAgent ([VillagerAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05720v1)), with a completion condition code can check, a neutral preview written by code, and bounded repair. The compiled tasks then reach the workers as menu options.

Who proposes matters. SELFGOAL's goal trees helped 7B models in negotiation games, but a weaker model building the tree hurt, GPT-4 beating GPT-3.5 by about three points ([SELFGOAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.04784)), and procedural memory written by a stronger model transferred to a weaker one ([MemP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06433)). Projects belong to the mayor on gpt-oss-120b, called rarely: EnvGen's handful of planning calls beat a GPT-4 agent that needed about 2,700 calls and $270 an episode ([EnvGen](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12014v2)). Ranking proposals by learnability and by novelty against the archive keeps them interesting yet achievable. Two risks need code answers. Agents inflate completion: AI Village agents claimed over 50 benchmark tests done when only a fraction were and inflated milestones into a "Historic Summit" ([AI Village](https://aivillageblog.substack.com/p/what-we-learned-2025); [timeline](https://theaidigest.org/village/timeline)), and Anthropic's long-running coding harness keeps its feature list in JSON, which models overwrite less readily than Markdown, letting agents flip only a `passes` field after end-to-end tests ([Anthropic](https://anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)). And proposal power becomes authority: in an 18-agent economy, decision authority tracked how often an agent proposed (standardised coefficients 0.82 and 0.78), not how much it talked or earned ([CAREB-MAS](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.23764v1)). If only the mayor proposes, the mayor's taste becomes the village's; a proposal slot for each worker, through the same validator, spreads agency. Cursor's planners that create tasks, workers that complete them and a judge that checks are the industrial form of the same structure ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)).

## Journals work when code selects and the strong model curates (approach 4)

Generative Agents remains the reference design for a memory journal. Each record is scored by recency (decay 0.995 per game hour), an LLM-rated importance from 1 to 10 and embedding relevance; reflection fires when recent importance sums past **150**, two or three times a game day, reading the 100 latest records, asking three questions and writing five insights that cite their evidence ([Generative Agents](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)). In its ablation, believability rankings by 100 people fell from 29.89 for the full design to 26.88 without reflection and 21.21 without memory, but that measured interview answers rather than competence, the study cost "thousands of dollars", and agents grew overly cooperative, embellished memories and chose odder places as memory grew ([Generative Agents](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)). Two details matter for you. Recency needs a clock that moves, and your world's time is frozen, so the journal needs a code-kept day. And the expensive parts, an importance rating per observation and a "should I react?" call each step, are what your typed events and chores already replace.

The memory-system literature carries a direct warning about your executor. LightMem's ICLR 2026 paper ran the main memory systems on **Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507**: on LoCoMo, plain full history scored **74.87**, LightMem 72.60, A-MEM 56.10, MemoryOS 51.30 and Mem0 **43.31** ([LightMem](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18866)). Even Mem0's own paper, on GPT-4o-mini, has full context ahead (72.90 against 66.88); its gains are tokens and latency ([Mem0](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19413)). Memory managed by the model, extracting facts and issuing add, update and delete calls, is fragile on your executor, so selection should be code's: typed events with code-assigned importance (a failure, a first success, a new place or a chat naming the agent high; routine chore steps low), recency on the village clock, and embedding relevance from a small local model if needed. More memory is not better. In Vending-Bench, larger memory windows made GPT-4o mini worse and breakdowns did not track a full context (r = 0.167) ([Vending-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15840v1)); Gemini's Pokémon agent repeated actions past ~100k tokens and fixated on a hallucinated goal written into its notes ([dbreunig](https://www.dbreunig.com/2025/06/17/an-agentic-case-study-playing-pokémon-with-gemini.html)); and in a 16-day multi-agent study, adversarial content written to persistent memory was acted on up to **46 hours** later ([Emergence World, Sept 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17320)). Entries need validating against world state, and an expiry. Cheap mechanisms with small-model evidence exist: Lyfe's Summarize-and-Forget drops a memory too similar to a stored one and clusters and summarises old ones ([Lyfe Agents](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.02172v1)); A-MEM's linked notes cost 1.1-1.4k tokens a write and roughly doubled multi-hop recall for 1-3B models, with linking doing most of the work in its ablation ([A-MEM](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12110v11)); and LightMem writes records cheaply during play and consolidates offline at "sleep time", using ~112k tokens and ~144 calls on a benchmark where other systems used 1.1-3.0M tokens and 520-2,938 calls ([LightMem](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18866)).

Lessons, the journal's most valuable layer, depend on who writes them. Reflexion gave no gain with a weak model, and its authors call self-correction "an emergent quality of stronger, larger models" ([Reflexion](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.11366)); ExpeL had GPT-4 extract insights that GPT-3.5 then acted on, lifting ALFWorld success from 40% to **59%**, with each insight kept by vote counts and deleted at zero ([ExpeL](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.10144v3)). ACE stores lessons as bullets with helpful and harmful counters merged by code, and showed why rewriting is dangerous: a baseline that rewrote its whole context in one pass shrank it from **18,282 to 122 tokens** and fell from 66.7% to 57.1% accuracy, below doing nothing (63.7%) ([ACE](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04618v3)). ReasoningBank found failures worth keeping as pitfalls (49.7 against 46.5 from successes alone) with a single retrieved item ([ReasoningBank](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25140v2)), and storing an agent's own successful trajectories as examples raised ALFWorld from 73% to 89%, more than upgrading gpt-4o-mini to gpt-4o ([Sarukkai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00234)). The journal that fits has two layers: code-written records plus an exact per-agent, per-activity table of attempts, successes, minutes and yield (ExpeL's counters, done in code), and at most ten lesson bullets per agent, curated each night by gpt-oss through add, update and delete edits that code applies, never a whole rewrite. One or two similar past choices with their outcomes then go into the menu prompt as examples.

Emergent roles fall out of the same loop. Project Sid's 30 identically prompted agents became farmers, miners, engineers, guards, explorers and blacksmiths only when they could form profiles of each other's goals, through a prompt section headed "Here's what other people are doing"; without it, roles were homogeneous and did not persist ([Project Sid](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00114v1)). A 2026 economy study found specialisation "largely LLM-native", locking in at 0.972, and faster at 0.985 with complementary skills ([CAREB-MAS](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.23764v1)), and even identical Llama-2-7B agents drifted into distinct personalities through interaction alone ([Takata et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.03252)). Roles themselves rarely break: across 1,600+ traces of multi-agent systems, role violations were 1.5% of failures, against 15.7% for step repetition and 12.4% for not knowing when to stop ([MAST](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3)). No study compares emergent and assigned roles on the same embodied task, so the design is a judgement call: keep the mayor and worker assignment for construction, infer after-phase roles each night from each agent's activity histogram, have the reflector name them and show them back ("you have become the village's miner"), let the experience counters reinforce them, and let falling gauges pull neglected work back onto everyone's menu.

## Typed acts and secret ballots beat free chat (approach 5)

The literature is close to unanimous that free chat between agents costs more than it returns. In MineCollab, requiring agents to communicate detailed plans cut performance by over 15%, crafting and cooking fell from up to 90% to under 30% going from two agents to five, and agents undid each other's work ([MineCollab](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.17950v1)). On the agent-only network Moltbook, 65% of comments shared no distinguishing vocabulary with the post they answered, an LLM judge labelled 28% spam, and only 5% were threaded replies ([Shekkizhar & Earle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20059)); individuals barely adapted to partners and no stable consensus formed, which the authors put down to the lack of shared social memory ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14299)). Your 553 messages in ten minutes before the 30-second limit were the same pathology. Central structure wins wherever it was compared: with 7B models, centralised control beat decentralised on building (0.42 against 0.00) and farming (0.36 against 0.00), with redundancy of 0.01 against 0.15 ([TeamCraft](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.05255)); a designated leader made GPT-3.5 trios about 10% faster, while leaders elected by GPT-4 agents saved steps but raised talk from 55 to 135 tokens a step, and GPT-3.5 agents could not run elections at all ([Guo et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12482v2)). Mechanical flood control is what works in Mineflayer practice: Mindcraft allows one bot-to-bot conversation at a time, waits 5 s before replying to a busy partner and doubles a 30 s nudge interval ([Mindcraft](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mindcraft-bots/mindcraft/main/src/agent/conversation.js)), and Gated Coordination (2026) escalates to teammates only when local recovery fails, weighed by criticality and cost ([Gated Coordination](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18975)).

The evidence therefore points to typed social acts rather than more chat: request, offer, accept, decline, report done, propose and vote. Code routes each act to its addressee, as MetaGPT's role subscriptions route documents ([MetaGPT](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.00352v6)); code closes each one (accepted, done, expired), giving every exchange the termination condition whose absence MAST found behind 12.4% of failures; and code checks any claim about the world against state before others see it, because in the AI Village one agent's invented 93-person contact list spread to every other agent through sycophantic agreement and cost 8+ hours ([AI Village](https://aivillageblog.substack.com/p/what-we-learned-2025)). No study has compared typed acts with free chat head to head. A commitment ledger is worth adding: in 2026 experiments with repeated games, deviations from announced actions were mostly planned in private (over 90% in the most deceptive conditions) and models disagreed on whether an announcement binds ([Shi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05132)), while gossip about past conduct raised cooperation in public-goods games ([Cross et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22049)). Asking for help is the act most likely to pay off for weak executors, since a GPT-4 expert's advice raised open models' Minecraft success by 4-38 points ([MindForge](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.12977v6)); an "ask the mayor" act puts gpt-oss behind the workers' hardest moments. Free speech can stay for your benefit as a watcher if it is conditioned on the decision: Project Sid's controller broadcast each decision to its talking module so agents stopped saying yes while doing something else ([Project Sid](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00114v1)).

For meetings and votes, the decision studies are blunt. Majority voting accounts for most of the gains attributed to multi-agent debate ([Choi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.17536)); voting protocols improved reasoning tasks by 13.2% where consensus helped knowledge tasks by 2.8%, and more discussion rounds before a vote hurt ([Kaesberg et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19130)); debate tips groups into collective bias once conformity passes a threshold, which heterogeneous agents suppress ([Okawa](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02827)); and the voting method and option order changed LLM outcomes ([Yang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01766)). A village meeting should be one round: code sets a short agenda, collects every proposal before anyone sees the others (the 60-100% first-proposal bias again), shows them shuffled with neutral code-written previews, takes one statement per agent and a secret simultaneous ballot, and enforces the result. Project Sid's tax law shows enforcement working: when amendments cut the tax from 20% to 5-10%, payments fell from about 20% to about 9%, and a frozen constitution kept rates constant ([Project Sid](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00114v1)). Emergence World's Town Hall (any agent proposes, 70% approval passes) suited Claude, whose ten agents all survived 15 days with 58 proposals and 32 constitutional articles, while the GPT-5-mini world held no votes and lost every agent ([Emergence World](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08367v1)): cheaper models will not govern themselves, so code must call meetings and carry out what they decide.

Shared resources need a harder line. In GovSim, 43 of 45 model-scenario runs collapsed a shared resource and every open model up to 72B survived **0%** of seeds; talking about the shared decision mattered (overuse rose 22% without it) and a "what if everyone did this?" line raised efficiency 24% ([GovSim](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16698v4)). A pre-stocked treasury raised survival under fatal scarcity from 2.5% to 27.5% ([GovSim-SelfGovern](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.22600v2)), power asymmetry cut survival by up to 87.3% ([SovSim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29062)), and reasoning models free-rode in public-goods games ([Guzman Piedrahita et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.23276)), which matters because gpt-oss is one. Anthropic's shop agent handed out discounts under pressure, its authors found helpfulness training made the agents decide like "a friend who just wants to be nice", and a same-model "CEO" agent added to oversee it was "not much help" ([Project Vend](https://www.anthropic.com/research/project-vend-1); [phase two](https://www.anthropic.com/research/project-vend-2)); an AgentVerse agent in Minecraft destroyed a village library for materials ([AgentVerse](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.10848v3)). Felling quotas, replanting, reserves and storage access therefore stay in code, with models voting on their parameters. GovSim's MIT code runs open models through vLLM, so its commons sub-skill tests can be run on qwen and gpt-oss first; Concordia's Ollama wrapper lets you rehearse a meeting against qwen offline before wiring it into the server ([GovSim code](https://github.com/giorgiopiatti/GovSim); [Concordia](https://github.com/google-deepmind/concordia)); and AI Town, an MIT-licensed TypeScript town that runs on Ollama by default, is the codebase nearest your stack for reading a conversation and memory loop ([AI Town](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town)).

## Rhythm and culture must be built, not awaited (approaches 6 and 7)

No study has tested schedules, sleep or set meeting times for Minecraft LLM agents, and none has studied village culture beyond seeded content. Both ideas still have clear borrowings, and both serve needs your models have.

A rhythm is the natural home for the slow work the other approaches require. Generative Agents plan each day from a summary of the previous one ([Generative Agents](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)); CitySim places mandatory blocks first and fills the rest at run time ([CitySim](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21805)); GovSim runs each "month" as strategy, harvest and moderated discussion ([GovSim](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16698v4)); and Emergence World runs on real time with day/night cycles and diaries ([Emergence World](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08367v1)), while Smart Zoi's end-of-day revision is the shipped example. The functional case is stronger than the precedent. Offline consolidation is far cheaper than consolidation during play (LightMem above; sleep-time compute cut test-time compute about fivefold ([Lin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13171))). Fresh starts fight drift: Cursor still needed "periodic fresh starts to combat drift and tunnel vision" ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)), non-thinking Qwen3 models copy their own earlier errors ([Sinha et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.09677v3)), Claude's Pokémon agent resumed multi-hour sequences after context resets by reading its own notes ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)), drift by inaction (not stopping an activity once its purpose is met) exceeded drift by action ([Arike et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.02709v1)), and a fixed reminder schedule fought persona drift as well as an adaptive one ([Leins et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24532)). With time frozen in your world, the day is a code clock, or the daylight cycle switched back on, which is harmless in peaceful mode. At dawn each agent reads its journal summary and the gauges, and the mayor gets the village report; by day agents choose at chore boundaries; at dusk a one-round meeting runs; at night gpt-oss reflects, curates lessons, infers roles and writes the chronicle while every context starts fresh. Night need not idle the bots, since DPT-Agent's reflection ran asynchronously and never delayed a decision ([DPT-Agent](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11882)). By our estimate, one nightly reflection per agent with a game day every 20-30 real minutes is 6-9 cloud calls an hour at 4-7k tokens each, a cost that grows with reflections, not with play time.

Culture has to be seeded and recorded rather than awaited. Project Sid's religion spread from 20 priests given that goal and was counted by keywords, and its memes were extracted by model calls ([Project Sid](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00114v1)), which critics called "a roundabout form of prompt engineering" ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=42035319)). Conventions do form fast: LLM populations converged on shared names by round 15 (all models tested but Llama-2), developed collective biases no single agent held, and were overturned by committed minorities of 2-67% depending on the model ([Ashery et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.08948v2)). In a group of three one persistent agent is 33%, above most measured tipping points, so the mayor, which proposes most, will name the places unless code balances who proposes. Content also spreads uncritically: hallucinated words and hashtags propagated through clusters of identical agents ([Takata et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.03252)). Rules have a published loop: CRSEC creates, spreads and evaluates norms and builds them into planning, and reduced social conflict in Smallville ([CRSEC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08251)); joined to GovSim-SelfGovern's laws as code, a village rule becomes a parameter over a tiny API (felling per area, replanting, withdrawal limits, quiet hours), proposed by any agent, previewed neutrally, voted, enforced by code and shown in every planning prompt. A chronicle answers Hughes et al.'s definition of open-endedness, a stream of artifacts that stays novel and learnable to an observer ([Hughes et al.](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/hughes24a.html)). The observer here is you, so gpt-oss can write a nightly entry from code-recorded events, judged for novelty against earlier entries, filling kinds of event not yet recorded rather than maximising a quality score, since AI quality scores stopped tracking human ratings at the top in QDAIF ([QDAIF](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13032v4)). Cap what culture can create: AI Village world-building weeks became an "expansion arms race" that broke a 3D hub under 13,750 added "phenomena" ([AI Village timeline](https://theaidigest.org/village/timeline)). And expect culture to vary with the model: in a cooperation game, only Claude 3.5 Sonnet societies improved across generations while GPT-4o's declined ([Vallinder & Hughes](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10270v1)).

## Score autonomy from the logs; keep humans for believability

No validated metric of agency or open-endedness exists for agent societies; the 2025-2026 proposals are taxonomies ([Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.13982)), and a 2026 critique warns that role-play plausibility is not behavioural validity and that outcomes can be artefacts of protocols and scheduling ([Li & Tao](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00113)). Most quantitative measures in this literature reduce to counting logged events, which a three-agent village can do for free if every decision is logged with the options offered, their facts, the choice, the reason and the facts it cites.

Three findings shape how to read these numbers. Long-running agents derail in every model: in Vending-Bench every model had bankrupt runs, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet's best run beat the human baseline while its worst failed after about 18 days ([Vending-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15840v1)); a GPT-4o agent's chance of succeeding on all eight tries of a tau-bench retail task was about 25%, some 60% below its single-try rate ([Sierra](https://sierra.ai/blog/benchmarking-ai-agents)). Report distributions, minimums and pass^k over seeded runs on a restored test site, which your rule of judging changes on more than three samples already anticipates. The AI Village's failure list (fabrication, found in 64 of 109,000 reasoning summaries; goal neglect; false completion; metric gaming; compulsive monitoring; an idle-nudge system that "misfired repeatedly") is a ready checklist for reading logs, and its nudges argue for firing on measured stalls, as your stall rules do, rather than on timers ([AI Village](https://aivillageblog.substack.com/p/what-we-learned-2025); [timeline](https://theaidigest.org/village/timeline)). Judges are weakest where it matters most: GPT-4 tracked humans on goal completion and material outcomes but less on believability ([Sotopia](https://iclr.cc/media/iclr-2024/Slides/17897.pdf)), so a qwen judge cannot stand in for the human ranking Generative Agents needed 100 raters for. Everitt et al. define goal-directedness as the propensity to use available capabilities toward a goal, distinct from performance and only moderately moved by motivational prompts ([Everitt et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11844v1)), which yields a concrete agency test: when a chosen goal needs iron and storage holds iron, does the agent withdraw it or mine again?

The protocol follows from the ablations in Generative Agents and Project Sid: scripted chores (today's baseline and the fallback), then the menu, then the menu with the journal, then gauges and personalities, then social acts and projects, each over several seeds, with a small human ranking of replays at the end. Logging every decision also keeps fine-tuning open: NVIDIA estimates 40-70% of the calls in existing agent frameworks could move to fine-tuned small models ([Belcak et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.02153v3)), an 8B model tuned on MineCollab's trajectories matched a 70B model on construction ([MineCollab](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.17950v1)), and a Mindcraft model was fine-tuned on one RTX 3090 in about three weeks ([Andy-4](https://huggingface.co/Sweaterdog/Andy-4)).

## Conclusion

The seven ideas are less alternatives than layers of one loop, and the menu is the only place any of them touches a local model: gauges and personalities set its scores, projects and requests arrive as new options, rules and failure caps remove options, and the journal annotates options with what each agent has learned. That nearly forces the build order (menu and decision logging first, gauges and the code-written journal next, typed acts and the clock after, cloud projects, curated lessons and culture last) and means most of the work is code rather than prompting: feasibility filters, counters, ledgers, validators and a clock. The binding constraint for your models is not the 8k window, of which a full menu prompt by our estimate uses less than half, but carrying state and judging feasibility, which is exactly what code can hold for them. A corollary worth testing early is that agency for the workers will come from proposal slots and a request channel rather than better prompts: in a group of three, whoever proposes sets the agenda, the conventions and in the end the culture.

The village would also fill a gap the literature names again and again: no published study runs ~30B local models as autonomous agents in an embodied multi-agent world for hours, compares a code-built menu with free goal generation, or tests needs-driven choice against a scripted priority list on real outcomes. Your restored test sites, scripted workers and staged runs are the controlled apparatus those comparisons lack, so the first ablation series, scripted chores against the menu against the menu with journal and gauges, is both the engineering test and a result the literature does not yet contain.
