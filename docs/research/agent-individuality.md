# Making individual agents from one model

Session 32, 2026-10-16. A research note for A.3 (gauges and personalities), E.3-E.6 (awareness, controller, reflection, ballots) and E.8 (specialisation). Every agent runs gpt-oss-120b, so any difference between Mayor, Worker1 and Worker24 has to come from what code gives them. This note builds on PLAN.md's rule 11 and the s26 review (Han et al., Newsham & Prince, MineLand, Riedl, Persistent Personas, Leins et al., D2A, Humanoid Agents), and does not repeat them except where new numbers change the picture.

## What the evidence says

**Persona prompts move what a model says about itself far more than what it does.** The Personality Illusion study (12 models) found only about 24% of trait-to-behaviour associations significant, and of those 52% pointed the way human data would, against 50% by chance. An agreeableness persona raised self-reported agreeableness strongly (beta about 3.6-4.4) but moved sycophancy behaviour by beta -0.05 to 0.32, mostly not significant; a self-regulation persona did not move risk-taking at all ([Han et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.03730)). BehaviorBench (IJCAI 2026) tied each Big Five trait to everyday behavioural markers and found the same mismatch ([BehaviorBench](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2026/658)). In annotation tasks, persona variables explain under 10% of the variance in human answers, so persona prompting can only add that much ([Hu & Collier](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10811)).

**Personas collapse when a task has a right answer, and survive where it has none.** With 15 socioeconomic personas, GPT-5's answers to SAT maths items showed no persona structure at all (PERMANOVA R2 0.0004), while on preference items every model kept clear persona differences (effect sizes 0.52-0.58) ([Two-faced agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.15573)). Across ten models, agents given distinct profiles converged into a narrow population; the models most faithful to each persona were the most stereotyped, coverage shrank from base to SFT to RL training, and thinking modes did not help ([Chameleon's Limit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.24698)). gpt-oss is a heavily RL-trained reasoning model, so expect the strongest pull toward one helpful-assistant mode.

**Where personas did change behaviour, the trait was a direct lever on the decision itself.** Agreeableness was the main driver of cooperation in repeated Prisoner's Dilemma for GPT-3.5, GPT-4o and GPT-5, other traits had little effect, and the authors call personality "a behavioural bias, not a deterministic control" ([Personality steering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05302)). In 1,500 negotiation runs, low agreeableness and openness gave "rational" play and high openness and conscientiousness fairer play ([Noh & Chang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05248)). In a Minecraft-like game-testing tool, an aggressive persona spent 21.5% of its actions on combat while a cautious one avoided combat entirely ([MIMIC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01635)); high-openness agents explored more in 25 text games ([Persona Dynamics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.06868)); MBTI priming gave analytic agents more stable game strategies ([MBTI-in-Thoughts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04343)). GovSim's one persona experiment is the strongest: a single greedy newcomer cut a GPT-4o community's survival from 53% to 33% and equality from 94 to 72, although in one trajectory it lowered its catch after the others talked norms with it ([GovSim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16698)). The pattern: a persona works when it names the very choice ("you like exploring" before an explore option) and that choice has no correct answer. Our chore menu, where every option is useful, is such a choice; executor turns and building are not, and personas will vanish there, which is fine.

**Ballots are closer to questionnaires than to actions.** That makes personas show easily in votes. Varying personas reduced some LLM voting biases and brought outcomes closer to human ones, while voting method and option order also changed results ([Yang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01766)); persona prompts predicted individual European Parliament votes at weighted F1 about 0.79 ([Kreutner et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11798)). The risk is the reverse of the chores: an agent that votes "in character" but works out of character. The vote-behaviour gap should be measured, not assumed away.

**What drifts.** Persona fidelity falls within about eight dialogue rounds ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10962)) and toward the default over 100 rounds for every model, scale reducing but not removing it ([Persistent Personas](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12775)); behaviour-specific reminders cut drift far more than re-injecting the persona ([Leins et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24532)). When agents rewrite their own identity at reflection, it drifts from the original even with an immutable "soul file" beside it, and in one direction regardless of how often reflection fires ([MicroVerse](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.15844)). Prompt-only personas drift more than fine-tuned ones in long civic simulations ([Point of Order](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.17813)). So the core of an identity should live in code and never be rewritten by the model; the model may add notes beside it.

**How agent societies got distinct individuals.** Mostly not from personality. Project Sid gave all 30 agents *the same* traits; roles (farmer, miner, guard, curator) came from social awareness and the community goal, and without the social module roles "did not persist ... and were homogeneous" ([Sid](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00114)). Takata et al. started agents with no traits at all and saw distinct personalities form through interaction and memory ([Takata et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.03252)). Generative Agents seeded each agent with one paragraph and let memory, reflection and relationships do the rest ([Park et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03442)); Humanoid Agents added needs, emotion and closeness, but the model judged need satisfaction poorly ([Humanoid Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05418)); Concordia frames every decision as "what kind of person am I, and what would such a person do here" over a formative memory ([Concordia](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03664)). Affordable Generative Agents found agents "can only generate finite behaviours in fixed environments" ([AGA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02053)): variety needs options. With only a fixed speaking order, strong models invented their own roles and abstained from tasks outside their competence, while weaker models did better with assigned structure ([Dochkina](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28990)); explicit social learning raised a role-specialisation index from about 0.6 to 0.85 ([M-S2L](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.18515)). Heterogeneity pays: two diverse agents (different models, prompts or tools) matched sixteen identical ones ([Agent scaling via diversity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03794)).

**Games put personality in the numbers.** The Sims scores each object's "advertisement" by the Sim's needs and multiplies by personality (a playful Sim weights the pinball machine over the bookcase), then picks randomly among the top few ([Sims AI transcript](https://amara.org/videos/cVoJS4OdmVql/en/4330642)); the same idea is utility AI's response curves ([utility systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_system), [IAUS](https://gameai.com/iaus.php)). The Sims 4's warning: players complain traits "almost don't make any difference" to autonomy, and mods exist to make them count ([Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1222670/discussions/0/501693985385917376), [Personality Please](https://snootysims.com/wiki/sims-4/personality-please/)): weights too small to see are wasted. RimWorld's passions set learning at 35%, 100% or 150% of base experience with a mood bonus for the work, skills above level 10 decay without practice, and backstories make some work impossible ([Passion](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Passion)). Dwarf Fortress gives every dwarf a liked stone, metal and gem, and better work from liked materials ([Preferences](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/Preferences)), plus 0-100 facets shown only at the extremes ([Personality](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/Personality)). Minecraft's own villagers take their profession from the job-site block they claim, so identity is a place ([Villager](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Villager)); Stardew's villagers are told apart by where they are at what hour and what gifts they love ([Abigail](https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Abigail)). The biology version is the variable response threshold model: identical workers specialise because a task's threshold falls each time a worker does it and rises while it neglects it ([Theraulaz et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1688885)).

What sits in code and what in the model is the main design choice: rows 1, 2, 4, 5, 8 and 9 work even in the fixed picker, so they give individuality at zero model cost and survive persona collapse. Rows 3, 6 and 7 are where the model adds colour and reasons.

## Proposed minimal design

Build in this order, each up the ladder (typecheck, staged after-phase from a checkpoint with the fixed picker, then a model-driven run):

1. **Temperaments in code (row 1).** 25 fixed vectors stored with the agent names (Worker5 is always Worker5), drawn to spread over the space (a Latin-hypercube draw, so code guarantees the population coverage the Chameleon study found models will not give), with the Mayor's set by hand. Weights large enough to see: the most-liked kind at about 2.5x neutral, the least-liked at 0.4x. Applied before the trim in choose.ts so the fixed picker shows the effect without any model. A switch `MCAI_TEMPERAMENT=off|on|identical` for E.8, which must run its (a) and (b2) arms with identical temperaments, as Sid did.
2. **The persona line and the own-pattern reminder (row 3),** written by code from the vector and the ledger, in every menu and ballot prompt. One sentence each, quantified where possible ("you choose exploring 3 times as often as farming"), never model-written. About 60 tokens: at 30 decisions an agent-hour for 25 agents, under 50k input tokens an hour, 2.5% of the hourly budget.
3. **Skill from practice (row 2).** The weight from practice and neglect plus the facts line, with the passion from the temperament. Leave any real in-world bonus (Haste for practised miners) for a later, separate test, since it changes the economy.
4. **Stakes and likes (rows 4-5),** when growth assigns houses and E.6 adds personal chests: home, own pen or field, liked wood and flower in the house trim, and stake facts in each ballot ("option B builds next to your house"; "the levy takes 20% of your logs").
5. **Ballot wiring.** The ballot call gets the persona line, the reminder, the stake facts and the agent's own record on the topic (how often it fells, mines, explores). Code also computes a "temperament-predicted vote" per agent and option (a fixed utility), logged beside the real vote but never shown.

## How to measure it

All from the choice ledger and ballot log, by run, against the null picker with temperaments on and off, so the code's share and the model's share separate:

- **Distinctness:** per-agent chore-mix entropy H(kind | agent) against the village's H(kind), and E.8's specialisation index I(agent; kind) / H(kind) against a label-shuffle null; mean pairwise Jensen-Shannon divergence between agents' kind histograms. Identical against distinct temperaments is the A/B (A.3's exit).
- **Manipulation check per trait:** rank correlation across agents between a kind's temperament weight and its share of that agent's minutes. Model mode against the fixed picker shows whether the model amplifies, keeps or flattens the code's tilt (flattening is persona collapse).
- **Model-only effect:** E.8's condition (d), persona lines on with code weights equalised: the same correlation measures what the line alone does.
- **Consistency over time:** split-half correlation of each agent's histogram (first against second half of a run, and across runs from the same checkpoint); persistence of the modal kind over windows of 5 decisions.
- **Identifiability:** a simple classifier (multinomial naive Bayes over kinds and option features) predicts the agent from a window of 10 choices; report accuracy against 1/25 chance, as browser agents were fingerprinted from their traces ([Fingerprinting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14786)). A second, batched check: an LLM judge given an agent's 30-minute log picks its persona line from 4.
- **Say-do and vote-do gaps:** the share of `say` lines that match the chosen option (E.4 already filters them), and agreement between each ballot and the temperament-predicted vote, then whether behaviour follows the vote (an agent that voted for a felling quota fells within it).
- **The user's test:** watch three agents for ten minutes in-game and name them by behaviour. If the user cannot, the weights are too small.

## Not worth it here

- **Activation or persona-vector steering** ([Persona vectors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.21509)) and **fine-tuned characters** ([Character-LLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10158)): need model weights; the cloud model gives none.
- **Long backstories, MBTI types or PersonaHub-style personas** ([PersonaHub](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.20094)): they move questionnaire answers and wording (what role-play benchmarks such as [PersonaGym](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18416) score), cost tokens in every call, and collapse under task load. One code-written line does the visible part.
- **Model-rewritten identities** at reflection: MicroVerse shows directional drift even beside an immutable core. Notes beside a fixed core only.
- **Letting the model judge needs or mood** (Humanoid Agents' F1 0.66-0.74): code computes gauges and thresholds.
- **Emotions as moods that swing:** in a peaceful world with no damage nothing drives them except chat, so they add noise, not character.
- **Temperature per agent as the source of individuality:** sampling changed wording, not behaviour (AGA), and it confounds diversity measurements.
- **Per-agent reasoning levels as a personality:** reasoning shifts cooperation ([Corrupted by Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.23276)), which makes it an experimental factor at most, not a trait; it also costs about 2.5x.
- **Different models per agent:** the strongest diversity lever in the literature, but it breaks the one-model base assumption and makes every comparison a model comparison.
- **Free chat to grow personalities** (Takata et al.): it worked, but at the cost MineCollab and lesson 4 warn about; typed acts and code-kept profiles give the same social memory.
- **Agents generated fresh each run:** fixed temperaments per name make runs comparable and let the user learn who is who.
